What the paper needs before it can run as a simulation

Review of the revised skeleton, read against what a facilitated multi-stakeholder exercise needs. Items marked “stand-in” are placeholders built into this prototype that the team should replace with its own content.

Missing

10
  1. The fourth quadrant. Two axes make four futures. The paper describes the two diagonal corners plus a “mixed” middle, so high cooperation + externally driven and low cooperation + SIDS-led are undefined. The second is arguably the most relevant future for SSTrC. The prototype now folds both into Fragmented Patchwork, so the model has three scenarios. The team should decide whether the second one deserves its own scenario.
  2. Written decision points. The paper says decision points become participant choices, but none are specified. Each needs an actor, a time window, two or three options and a stated trade-off. The 81 options here are stand-ins.
  3. A quantified starting state. No baseline for the protection gap, pre-arranged finance share, debt service or pool coverage by region. Without one, outcomes can’t be scored credibly.
  4. Role briefs. Each actor needs a mandate, resources, constraints and red lines. Without conflicting interests, players pick the “good” option every time. One-line interests are stand-ins here.
  5. The UN system beyond UNOSSC. UNDP, UNDRR, OCHA, the UNFCCC secretariat, UN-OHRLLS and UNIDO are not in the audience list, and the division of labour with UNOSSC is not described.
  6. Adjudication rules. Nothing says how choices turn into outcomes: a model, facilitator judgment or a mix. This prototype uses a simple additive model that needs validation.
  7. Named signposts. Signposts appear as headings in sections 4 and 5 but no indicator, data source or threshold is listed.
  8. A last-mile measure and voice. Last-mile delivery is the through-line but has no indicator, and communities appear only as recipients. Gender and inclusion are absent.
  9. Debt as a driver. Debt deepens in Scenario 2 and constrains every SIDS choice, but it is not among the five drivers.
  10. Wildcard mechanics. The wildcards are named but not timed, scaled or linked to what changes for each actor.

Needs improvement

8
  1. Define the MVI. Scenario 1 leaves open whether the MVI shapes eligibility, concessional terms or distribution. Pick one; the simulation currently assumes concessional terms.
  2. Place Scenario 2 on the axes. “Mixed, muddling through” has no coordinates. State where it sits, or replace it with the missing quadrants.
  3. Turn driver relationships into causal links. Section 3 describes how drivers relate in prose. A simulation needs direction and rough strength for each link.
  4. Move regional differences into the scenario logic. Caribbean, Pacific, AIS and micro-state differences sit under Limitations, yet Scenario 2 depends on them. This prototype splits SIDS into three playable roles.
  5. Give each stakeholder lens a stake. “What each actor faces” should include what each actor wants and loses, so alignment and conflict show up in play.
  6. Make data control an explicit choice. The technology driver hinges on who owns data. Frame it as a decision, not a trend.
  7. Confirm the audience. Section 2 still reads “Confirm with the team”. Roles, session length and language level depend on it.
  8. Add validation to the method. A small-team adaptation can be strengthened by playtesting with two or three interviewees before the full session.

To add

8
  1. Facilitator guide. Timings, rules, how to run negotiation between rounds, when to inject wildcards and how to debrief.
  2. Printable role cards. One page per actor: mandate, interests, resources, red lines, decision options.
  3. A round calendar tied to real milestones. E.g. the Sevilla follow-up, COP cycles, the 2030 SDG deadline and the end of the Antigua and Barbuda Agenda for SIDS period in 2034.
  4. A negotiation phase. Time between briefing and decisions for actors to form coalitions. Linked decisions (e.g. founding an AIS pool needs AIS states, funders and regional institutions together) reward it.
  5. An effects annex. A table of every option’s effects on the two axes and indicators, so the model is transparent and editable.
  6. A structured debrief. Link outcomes to Section 5: which no-regret moves were made, which ESB recommendations held, which signposts appeared first.
  7. Exercise evaluation. Short pre/post questions on participants’ views of SSTrC and pre-arranged finance, to show the exercise’s value to UNOSSC.
  8. Data sources per indicator. Where each baseline and signpost value comes from, flagged against the SIDS data gaps in Limitations.

How can SSTrC help SIDS access, adapt and scale pre-arranged disaster-risk financing toward 2040?

Stakeholders make decisions across three periods. Their choices move two critical uncertainties, international cooperation and SIDS agency, and the end position determines which 2040 scenario arrives. UNOSSC convenes; it is not the only decision-maker.

01Facilitator reads the briefing and signposts. 02Stakeholders negotiate. Some decisions only pay off when made together. 03Each role commits one decision. 04Resolve: see how the axes and indicators move. Wildcards can land at any time.
